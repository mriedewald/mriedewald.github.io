Inside the Noise: What Real-World Invoices Teach Us About Unstructured Data (February 28, 2026)

TL;DR

Real invoice messiness (layouts, handwriting, OCR errors) and data-quality challenges

Full Post

An invoice is not the product of a creative writing process, but rather a functional document that serves a specific purpose: to request payment for goods or services rendered. Many of the important fields appear on virtually every invoice, such as vendor name and address, customer name and address, invoice number and date, line items and amounts, etc. Hence one would expect them to have a clear structure and follow a standardized format. Unfortunately, this is usually not the case. In fact, when automatically analyzing and coding invoices, one is often better off treating them like messy unstructured documents instead of the template-based forms they ideally should be. We will discuss the dimensions of the problem and give concrete examples, which are abstracted from real customer data. Problem dimensions: Too much information: multiple invoices in the same file, boilerplate text with background info (like "how to read your utility bill") Missing information: important context is not on the invoice, e.g., details of the work done, or the latest status of payments How to extract the relevant information in a structured way: vendor name and address, invoice number, date, line items, amounts, etc.? How to use this information to code the invoice, i.e., assign the right account codes to the line items, and determine the right amount to pay for each line item? Basics: An invoice should be like a form with a fixed and standardized layout, with clearly labeled fields for the invoice number, date, customer name, line items, amounts, etc. Then one could simply read everything off, right? Wrong. First, invoices are often messy, unstructured, and full of noise. Second, the most important information for coding is not on the invoice, because the vendor who created it typically does not know it. Issues: odd layouts: person's regular and overtime hours changes in layout; changes in vendor codes; changes in properties managed handwritten notes (details, info crossed out), explaining what the person did, or why they worked overtime utility bill: delivery vs supply charges, gross receipts tax, some reimbursements, sales taxes for city, county, and state remittance vendors: same vendor name, but different addresses, phone numbers, and bank accounts OCR errors: "0" vs "O", "1" vs "I", "5" vs "S", etc. part of a contract or agreement: important context is missing; e.g., rebates or things that do not need to be paid but still appear on the invoice multiple invoices in the same document invoices plus an email exchange explaining the context: invoice shows an aggregate charge, but the email explains details of hourly rate, hours worked and jobs done invoice with aggregate info plus an attached receipt from a home improvement store showing the details of the line items unclear assignment of multiple line items to multiple accounts late fees, discounts, and other adjustments that are not clearly labeled; some customers do not pay the late fees, but there may be exceptions latest status of payments may not be reflected in the invoice, e.g., if the invoice was sent out a week ago, but the customer paid yesterday, the invoice may still show as unpaid invoice shows a due date, but there are terms like NET 30, which means that the due date is actually 30 days after the invoice date, not the due date shown on the invoice invoice number is shown on the invoice, but the customer may choose a different one, e.g., to make them unique or encode relevant information such as account number or project code and date. when to predict vs when/what to read off the invoice inconsistent or outdated auxiliary databases like for vendors and properties which examples explain best how to code a new given invoice?
